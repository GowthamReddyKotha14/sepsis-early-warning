Earlier sepsis warnings, without burying nurses in alarms.

An AI model trained on one hospital's ICU patients, then tested on 20,000 patients from a different hospital it never saw. Below, 16 of those real patients replay hour by hour: watch the risk move, see each alert fire, and read why it fired.

Choosing the alarm level

More alarms help, until nurses stop listening

The best score reachable at each alarm load, from every possible alarm level scored on hospital A's validation patients. The chosen level is the best score within a budget of alerts per bed per shift, the load the existing qSOFA rule already puts on nurses there.

How it's built

Choices that keep the numbers honest

A different hospital as the test

Trained and tuned on hospital A only. Hospital B is touched once, at the end. The challenge's own finding was that models degrade across hospitals, so this is the question that matters.

Nothing from the future

Every feature uses only what was known at that hour: last values carried forward, trends over the past 6 hours, hours since each lab. A test changes a patient's future and checks earlier features don't move.

The official score, verified

The challenge's utility rewards warnings 6 to 12 hours before onset and penalizes false alarms. Reimplemented for speed and checked against PhysioNet's own code on 400 random patients.

Alarms counted like nurses feel them

An alert is a warning switching on, counted per bed per 12-hour shift. The alarm level is capped at the load nurses already accept from qSOFA.

Every alert explained

For each alert, one measurement at a time is replaced with a typical healthy value; the ones that lower the risk most are the reasons, written with the patient's real values.

Today's rules as the bar

SIRS and qSOFA are what many hospitals screen with now. On hospital B, SIRS alarms constantly and scores below zero.
